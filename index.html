The Approach

We are not a biosensor company.

Designing a new glucose-sensing element is a multi-year, capital-intensive, regulatory-heavy undertaking — and doing early iterations of that on a living animal is a real safety problem, not a corner worth cutting. Aegis's engineering goes into the layer above the sensor.

Every sensor in our platform is an already-manufactured, already-cleared product designed for human use — applied thoughtfully to dogs, not reinvented from zero. Our own work is the calibration engine, the trend analysis, the hypoglycemia early-warning system, and the bridge hardware that connects proven sensors into one coherent platform.

That's a systems-integration problem, and it's the one we're built to solve well: take what already works in human medicine, verify it rigorously, and make it accessible for the animals who need it too.

  • ✕ Inventing a new biosensor from scratch
  • ✓ Integrating FDA-cleared, mass-manufactured sensors
  • ✕ Locking owners into one expensive hardware path
  • ✓ A tiered ladder from free to full continuous monitoring
  • ✕ A dosing calculator that tells you what to give

Survival time after diagnosis — the honest, unresolved answer

Two real studies give meaningfully different numbers, and neither should be quoted alone: a UK primary-care study found a median survival of 15.6 months from diagnosis (20.2 months for dogs who survive the first week), while an Italian specialist veterinary-hospital study found a median of 964 days — roughly 2.6 years. The gap is likely explained by care setting and case mix, not a contradiction in the disease itself — general practice sees a broader, less-selected population than a referral hospital. The veterinary field itself has published on whether current data is even adequate to counsel individual owners — this isn't a number Aegis is smoothing over, it's an open question in veterinary medicine.

What actually moves the needle

Real, cited risk factors: age 10+, Cocker Spaniels specifically, high blood glucose at diagnosis, and prior steroid treatment are all associated with worse outcomes; neutered status and (in one study) Border Collies specifically were associated with better ones. Notably, diabetic ketoacidosis at diagnosis was not significantly associated with survival in the largest study reviewed — a genuinely scary-sounding presentation doesn't automatically mean a worse long-term outlook.

Who's more likely to be affected

A 2025 UK diabetes registry found dramatic breed skew: Samoyeds are roughly 23.5×, Tibetan Terriers 11×, and Dachshunds 11× more likely to develop diabetes than baseline. Unspayed female dogs are roughly 2× as likely as males — most cases are adult-onset, not tied to a heat cycle, but a real minority (~10%) are.
